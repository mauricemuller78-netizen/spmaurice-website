Bayern Munich 2019/20: Champions of Europe

Hansi Flick's 2019/20 Champions League-winning Bayern Munich. A tactical analysis of the structures, pressing and decisive ideas behind one of Europe's most dominant seasons.

In Possession

The following five frames show two different parts of Bayern's attacking game: the first image focuses on the build-up, while images two to five show examples of the team's most common in-possession structures.

1 / 5

Image 1 — Build-Up

One of Bayern's biggest strengths in 2019/20 was how easily they could start attacks through Manuel Neuer. As one of the best goalkeepers in possession, he gave Bayern solutions both under heavy pressure and when the direct option was available. Whether it was a short pass into a free teammate or a longer ball over the first line, Neuer consistently helped Bayern find the spare man and escape pressure.

The build-up itself contained a lot of rotation and was designed to move the ball forward quickly. Thomas Müller and Robert Lewandowski were especially important as reference points because both were extremely strong at receiving under pressure, protecting the ball and connecting the next action. The double pivot also gave Bayern control, with Thiago often at the centre of that phase. His ability on the ball made him a key player for escaping pressure and progressing possession.

Joshua Kimmich usually started from right-back, but his role was much more than that of a traditional full-back. Similar to Philipp Lahm in earlier Bayern teams, Kimmich acted like a playmaker from the flank and gave the team another intelligent distributor during the first and second phases of possession.

Images 2–5 — In-Possession Structures

Bayern most often attacked from a relatively classic 4-2-3-1. The full-backs pushed forward and overlapped, while the attacking players were given plenty of freedom to rotate around Lewandowski. The exact structure always depended on the personnel and the situation.

They could also form a 3-1-6, usually by having one of the holding midfielders or a full-back join the centre-backs to create the first line, with one player positioned in front of them and six players occupying the attacking line and half-spaces higher up the pitch.

More generally, Flick's Bayern were defined by a high level of positional freedom. The structure gave them a clear base, but the players were encouraged to rotate, move into different spaces and find the quickest route forward rather than staying in rigid positions. That freedom, combined with their technical quality, made Bayern extremely difficult to control once they progressed into the attacking half.
