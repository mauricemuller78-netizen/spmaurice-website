Bayern Munich 2019/20: Champions of Europe

Hansi Flick's 2019/20 Champions League-winning Bayern Munich. A tactical analysis of the structures, pressing and decisive ideas behind one of Europe's most dominant seasons.

SPMaurice2020 Champions League Winners

Season Overview

Bayern's 2019/20 season changed completely after Hansi Flick took over in November 2019. He initially stepped in on an interim basis after Niko Kovač left, with Bayern sitting fourth in the Bundesliga. The first weeks were not completely straightforward, but once Flick's ideas took hold the season became increasingly one-sided. Bayern climbed back to the top of the league, finished with 82 points and 100 Bundesliga goals, and ended the campaign 13 points clear of Borussia Dortmund.

The domestic dominance was matched in the cups. Bayern won the DFB-Pokal and then completed the treble by winning the Champions League. Their European run was especially dominant: Bayern won every match they played in the competition on the way to the trophy. What started as a season in which the team looked far from untouchable ended as one of the most complete campaigns in the club's history.

Build-Up

One of Bayern's biggest strengths in 2019/20 was how easily they could start attacks through Manuel Neuer. As one of the best goalkeepers in possession, he gave Bayern solutions both under heavy pressure and when the direct option was available. Whether it was a short pass into a free teammate or a longer ball over the first line, Neuer consistently helped Bayern find the spare man and escape pressure.

The build-up itself contained a lot of rotation and was designed to move the ball forward quickly. Thomas Müller and Robert Lewandowski were especially important as reference points because both were extremely strong at receiving under pressure, protecting the ball and connecting the next action. The double pivot also gave Bayern control, with Thiago often at the centre of that phase. His ability on the ball made him a key player for escaping pressure and progressing possession.

Joshua Kimmich usually started from right-back, but his role was much more than that of a traditional full-back. Similar to Philipp Lahm in earlier Bayern teams, Kimmich acted like a playmaker from the flank and gave the team another intelligent distributor during the first and second phases of possession.

In-Possession Structures

Bayern most often attacked from a relatively classic 4-2-3-1. The full-backs pushed forward and overlapped, while the attacking players were given plenty of freedom to rotate around Lewandowski. The exact structure always depended on the personnel and the situation.

They could also form a 3-1-6, usually by having one of the holding midfielders or a full-back join the centre-backs to create the first line, with one player positioned in front of them and six players occupying the attacking line and half-spaces higher up the pitch.

More generally, Flick's Bayern were defined by a high level of positional freedom. The structure gave them a clear base, but the players were encouraged to rotate, move into different spaces and find the quickest route forward rather than staying in rigid positions. That freedom, combined with their technical quality, made Bayern extremely difficult to control once they progressed into the attacking half.

2020 Champions League Final — Bayern Munich vs Paris Saint-Germain

Build-Up

Paris Saint-Germain did not press Bayern especially high during most of the build-up phase. When PSG did step forward, Manuel Neuer often looked to find the full-backs, most commonly Joshua Kimmich on the right.

From there, Kimmich could combine with Thomas Müller and, less frequently, Robert Lewandowski. Those combinations gave Bayern a way to progress down the right side without forcing the ball through the centre immediately.

Final Third

Higher up the pitch, Kimmich regularly moved inside. Sometimes he positioned himself higher, while in other moments he dropped deeper and exchanged positions with Leon Goretzka. This gave Bayern flexibility on the right side and changed the occupation of the half-space depending on the situation.

Serge Gnabry generally stayed wide on the right touchline, while on the left Alphonso Davies provided width and Kingsley Coman moved more inside. In the centre, Müller and Lewandowski had a large amount of freedom to move and combine between the lines.

Thiago also played an important role from deeper areas. He frequently looked for longer passes into the space behind PSG's defensive line, giving Bayern another way to attack directly when the shorter combinations were not available.

Without the Ball — High Pressing Against PSG's Build-Up

Bayern pressed PSG aggressively high during the build-up. The wingers jumped onto PSG's centre-backs, while Robert Lewandowski took responsibility for the number six and tried to block the central route into midfield.

PSG therefore often looked to escape towards the full-backs. As soon as the ball was moved wide, Thomas Müller shifted across and attacked the side where the ball went, helping Bayern continue the press instead of allowing PSG to settle possession on the flank.

One of PSG's clearest ways of progressing was through Neymar. He repeatedly dropped deeper on the left side to offer a connection and help carry PSG forward. When Neymar moved away from the last line to receive, Kylian Mbappé attacked the space created by that movement, giving PSG a direct threat in behind Bayern's high defensive structure.

Final Assessment — Bayern in Complete Control

Bayern were on another level in this final, both with and without the ball. In possession, they controlled the build-up through Manuel Neuer, used Joshua Kimmich as a key outlet on the right and created flexible combinations through Thomas Müller, Robert Lewandowski and the movements around them. Higher up the pitch, Kimmich's movement inside, Serge Gnabry holding the width on the right, Alphonso Davies providing width on the left and Kingsley Coman moving inside gave Bayern different ways to occupy PSG's defensive line. Thiago added another dimension with his ability to control deeper areas and play longer passes into the space behind.

Without the ball, Bayern were just as dominant. Their high press made PSG's build-up extremely uncomfortable: the wingers jumped onto the centre-backs, Lewandowski controlled the number-six space and Müller constantly shifted towards the side of the ball when PSG tried to escape through the full-backs. PSG's most dangerous solution came through Neymar dropping deeper on the left and Mbappé attacking the space created by that movement, but even those moments came inside a match that Bayern largely dictated through their intensity, positioning and collective control.

The final was therefore a perfect summary of this Bayern side. They could dominate through possession, attack quickly when the space opened and immediately put the opponent under pressure when the ball was lost. PSG had individual moments, but Bayern controlled the game on their terms in both phases.

My View

For me, what Flick did with this team is incredible. He took over a side that, at that point, did not even look certain to become Bundesliga champions and turned them into a treble-winning team. The tactical improvement was obvious, but the mental transformation was just as important.

Bayern began playing with a completely different level of confidence, aggression and belief. The team looked convinced that it could dominate every opponent, whether through the ball or through the press. That change in mentality is one of the biggest reasons why this Bayern side became so overwhelming. Flick did not simply improve the structure of the team — he changed the way the players seemed to see themselves.
