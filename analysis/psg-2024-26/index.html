PSG 2024–26: The Making of Back-to-Back European Champions

A tactical study of Paris Saint-Germain across the 2024/25 and 2025/26 seasons, exploring how Luis Enrique built a team capable of dominating Europe through pressing, fluid movement and collective organisation.

SPMaurice2026

Introduction

This analysis marks the beginning of a series looking at the UEFA Champions League winners of the past 20 years, with each edition focusing on the tactical identity and key principles behind a successful European campaign.

The series begins with Paris Saint-Germain and their remarkable period between 2024 and 2026. What makes this PSG side particularly interesting is not simply the success itself, but the level of dominance they were able to sustain across two consecutive seasons.

The central question of this analysis is therefore simple: How did PSG do it?

To answer that question, the analysis will look at the evolution of the team under Luis Enrique, their structures in possession and without the ball, the freedom and rotations within their attacking game, and the collective mechanisms that allowed those movements to function without losing balance.

Both Champions League finals will then be examined individually, before bringing the different elements together to understand why this PSG side was able to remain at the top of European football across two seasons.

The Evolution of PSG

Phase 1 — Building the Structure

In the early stages of Luis Enrique’s project, PSG played a highly controlled and positionally disciplined style of football. Players rarely exchanged positions freely, with the team instead maintaining clearly defined zones and a stable positional structure.

At this point, the priority was not yet to create the fluid and unpredictable PSG that would emerge later. Enrique first needed to build a functioning collective: establishing clear reference points in possession, teaching the players his positional principles and creating a shared understanding of how the team should occupy the pitch.

This controlled structure provided the foundation for PSG’s later evolution. Once the players had internalised Enrique’s ideas and developed stronger relationships with one another, the team could gradually move away from rigid positioning and introduce the rotations, freedom and collective adaptations that would eventually become central to their identity.

As the group developed, that began to change. PSG gradually became less rigid and more fluid. Players were given greater freedom to leave their positions, rotate with teammates and interpret space, because the team had developed the collective understanding required to compensate for those movements.

Several players became particularly important to that evolution. Ousmane Dembélé, Khvicha Kvaratskhelia, Nuno Mendes, Vitinha, Achraf Hakimi and Désiré Doué all had a major tactical influence on the team. Their specific roles will become clearer in the sections on PSG’s behaviour in and out of possession.

The 2024/25 season also represented a transition within the squad itself. Kvaratskhelia, for example, only arrived during the winter window, meaning the team that eventually won the Champions League was still developing during the season.

By 2025/26, PSG were also able to manage their squad more deliberately around the Champions League. Key players such as Dembélé, Doué, Vitinha and Nuno Mendes were used less frequently in domestic competitions, allowing them to arrive fresher for the biggest European matches. At the same time, PSG retained their most important players while continuing to add depth to the squad.

In Possession — From Structure to Freedom

Once PSG had established the positional foundations of Luis Enrique’s football, their possession game evolved into something far more fluid. The following sequences are examples of recurring rotations rather than fixed patterns. The positions constantly changed, but the underlying principle remained the same: when one player moved away from his zone, another player occupied or protected the space that movement created.

Right-Side Rotations

Much of PSG’s possession was developed through the right side. Dembélé could drop extremely deep on the right, drawing opponents away from the last line and becoming involved in the construction of the attack. His movement opened space ahead of him for Hakimi and, in this example, Doué to attack. Behind those rotations, Zaïre-Emery could cover the space vacated by Hakimi, allowing the right-back to advance without leaving the structure completely exposed.

Vitinha, Nuno Mendes & the Left Side

Vitinha’s positioning was equally flexible. He could operate as the number six, but when Nuno Mendes moved high and occupied the left wing, Vitinha could drop into the left centre-back zone. In those situations, Fabián Ruiz or Zaïre-Emery could become the central midfielder at the base of the structure. Rather than relying on one fixed build-up shape, PSG continually redistributed responsibilities depending on the movement around the ball.

Width Without Fixed Wingers

Kvaratskhelia often provided width on the left touchline, but even that role was not permanent. He could suddenly appear on the opposite touchline, just as Doué could move across the pitch. These exchanges made PSG difficult to reference defensively: the width remained, but the player providing it could change from one sequence to the next.

Occupying the Centre

The rotations could create unusual attacking pictures. At times, Fabián Ruiz could become the only PSG player occupying the highest central zone while the nominal forwards moved elsewhere to create or attack space. What looked like positional freedom was therefore not simply random movement — the team continued to occupy the necessary spaces, only with different players filling them.

Switching to the Weak Side

Although PSG frequently progressed through the right, the opposite side remained a constant threat. When Nuno Mendes and Kvaratskhelia were isolated on the left, PSG could quickly switch the ball across the pitch and attack the space created by the opponent’s shift towards the right. The result was a possession structure built around overloads, rotations and rapid changes of the point of attack rather than fixed individual positions.

Without the Ball

Without the ball, PSG were just as aggressive and flexible as they were in possession. The following clip shows two examples of opposition build-up structures PSG regularly had to solve and how their man-oriented pressing adapted to both.

Against a 4-4-2 Build-Up

Against a 4-4-2, the opposition’s two forwards could drop very deep while the wide players stayed high. PSG responded with an extremely aggressive man-oriented approach: the centre-backs stepped forward onto the two strikers and were prepared to follow them even when they moved far away from the defensive line.

For most teams, committing centre-backs this aggressively would create major problems elsewhere. PSG could sustain it because the pressing system had become deeply internalised. Players were comfortable taking over different responsibilities as the structure changed, allowing one player to leave his nominal position while teammates adjusted and protected the spaces around him.

Against a 4-3-3 Build-Up

The same principle could be adapted against a 4-3-3. In the example shown, the left centre-back steps onto the opposition striker, the right centre-back moves out towards the right winger, and the right-back jumps forward onto the opposition right-back. PSG’s defensive line therefore did not simply protect its own zones — individual defenders could leave the line and take responsibility for opponents much higher up the pitch.

This required enormous collective understanding. The pressing structure could change within seconds, but because players were capable of covering different positions and responsibilities, PSG could maintain pressure without abandoning the overall organisation.

Forcing Play Away from the Centre

The final seconds of the clip highlight an important detail in the forwards’ pressing. Rather than running directly towards the centre-backs, PSG’s forwards approached from the inside and curved their runs outwards. Their first objective was to close the passing lane into central areas; only then did they accelerate towards the centre-back.

This meant the press was not based on intensity alone. PSG used the angle of the run to remove the opponent’s preferred inside option, guide the build-up towards the outside and then attack the ball. The aggression of the press was therefore supported by a clear understanding of which spaces PSG wanted to protect and which passes they were willing to allow.

2025 Champions League Final

PSG’s 5–0 victory over Inter was a perfect demonstration of the principles that had defined their evolution under Luis Enrique. The five goals did not come from one fixed attacking pattern. Instead, they showed positional rotations, aggressive counter-attacking, technical combinations and players repeatedly appearing in spaces far away from their nominal positions.

Goal 1 · Hakimi

Rotations Expose Inter’s Marking

Inter already look uncomfortable in their pressing and marking. Dembélé stays extremely wide on the right, while Nuno Mendes, Pacho and Marquinhos form the back three. Doué moves inside from the right wing and effectively exchanges positions with Dembélé, immediately changing Inter’s reference points.

The decisive detail is Hakimi. Although nominally the right-back, he attacks all the way into the centre and arrives around the penalty spot to finish the move. The goal is a clear example of PSG’s positional freedom: the right-back becomes the central finisher while the attacking players around him create and occupy different spaces.

Goal 2 · Doué

Turning a Throw-In into a Transition

The second goal begins with an Inter throw-in. Pacho initially heads the ball towards the touchline, but PSG manage to keep it in play and immediately transform the situation into a counter-attack.

Dembélé and Kvaratskhelia accelerate down the left and the physical difference in transition becomes obvious. PSG attack the open pitch with far more speed than Inter can recover with. The move ends with Doué finishing an excellent transition — a goal that shows how quickly PSG could turn a seemingly harmless defensive situation into a decisive attack.

Goal 3 · Doué

Dembélé Drops, Doué Attacks the Space

The third goal is built through an excellent combination between Vitinha and Dembélé. Their quick short passing draws Inter towards the ball, while Dembélé drops deeper instead of occupying the last line.

That movement creates the space for Doué to attack. Vitinha recognises it and plays the decisive pass into him. Once Doué receives in the one-against-one, his technical quality makes the situation extremely difficult to defend. Again, the key is the rotation: Dembélé leaves the advanced space and Doué immediately becomes the player who attacks it.

Goal 4 · Kvaratskhelia

Build-Up, Depth and an Open Weak Side

The fourth goal starts from a more conventional build-up, but the positions are anything but conventional. Vitinha is already very high in the right-central half-space, while Dembélé makes another outstanding run in behind from a much deeper position.

The movement stretches Inter and opens a huge amount of space for Kvaratskhelia. This time he occupies the type of isolated attacking space Doué had exploited earlier. Once Kvaratskhelia receives with that much room and can attack the defender directly, Inter have very little chance of stopping him.

Goal 5 · Mayulu

Collective Movement Until the Final Goal

The fifth goal again begins through PSG’s build-up, this time on the right. Warren Zaïre-Emery makes an excellent run towards the right touchline to support Hakimi before carrying the ball diagonally far into the centre.

From there, PSG move the attack all the way to the opposite side and find Barcola on the left wing. Barcola combines quickly with Mayulu, whose movement completes the sequence and produces the fifth goal. Even at 4–0, PSG’s final goal contains the same ideas seen throughout the match: support around the ball, movement across zones, a rapid switch of the attacking point and technically clean combination play.

The five goals capture why PSG were so difficult to control. Hakimi could finish as a central attacker, Dembélé could move from the touchline into deeper areas, Doué could replace him in advanced zones, Vitinha could appear high between the lines and Zaïre-Emery could move from supporting the right side into a central dribble before the attack finished on the opposite wing. Inter were not defending a fixed formation — they were constantly being asked to solve new positional relationships.

2026 Champions League Final

The final showed a similar problem for both teams after falling behind: there was plenty of movement in possession, but not every movement created a real threat.

PSG: Overloads Without Penetration

After conceding, PSG increasingly overloaded the right side, with Kvaratskhelia moving across to create an extra option. Arsenal's defensive structure remained stable, however, because the rotations rarely forced a defender to leave his reference or opened a genuinely free player.

One of PSG's most promising moments came when Fabian Ruiz stayed wider than usual. His position stretched Arsenal horizontally and briefly created better spacing, but PSG did not reproduce the situation consistently enough to turn it into a recurring solution.

Second-Half Adjustment

From the start of the second half, PSG appeared to recognise that their earlier attacking patterns were not creating enough danger. The first five frames mainly reflect the first-half structure, but here the approach changes. PSG began trying to direct play towards the left side before using quick switches of play to attack the opposite flank. The idea was to manipulate Arsenal's defensive shape, draw pressure to one side and then create a more open situation on the far side. It was a clear adjustment aimed at finding a new route into the game when the initial approach was no longer working.

Why This Team Worked

PSG’s success was built on the balance between freedom and collective responsibility. Luis Enrique did not begin by giving the team complete positional freedom. He first established a clear structure, fixed reference points and a shared understanding of how the team should occupy the pitch. Only once those foundations were internalised did PSG become the fluid side seen in their title-winning seasons.

That evolution allowed individual players to move far beyond their nominal roles without breaking the team. Dembélé could drop into midfield, Hakimi could attack central spaces, Nuno Mendes could become the highest player on the left, Vitinha could move between midfield and the defensive line, and Doué or Kvaratskhelia could appear on either side of the attack. The important point was not simply that players rotated, but that the rest of the team understood how to compensate for those movements.

The same collective intelligence appeared without the ball. PSG’s man-oriented pressing was extremely aggressive, with defenders regularly leaving the back line to follow opponents into deeper areas. That approach only worked because the team was comfortable exchanging defensive responsibilities and protecting the spaces created by each individual jump.

The 2025 Champions League Final showed the system at its best. Inter were repeatedly forced to solve new positional relationships, while PSG combined rotations, direct transitions, weak-side switches and individual quality to create five different goals. The 2026 final also showed the other side of the model: when the usual rotations and overloads did not create enough penetration, PSG were still capable of adjusting the point of attack and searching for alternative solutions.

Ultimately, PSG were difficult to defend because opponents could not rely on fixed references. The structure remained, but the players occupying each space constantly changed. That combination of organisation, technical quality, athleticism and tactical freedom made the team both unpredictable in possession and extremely aggressive without it.

Conclusion

Between 2024 and 2026, Paris Saint-Germain developed from a controlled positional team into one of the most fluid and adaptable sides in Europe. The key was not abandoning structure, but using structure as the foundation for freedom.

With the ball, PSG created advantages through rotations, overloads, switches of play and players attacking spaces far away from their nominal positions. Without the ball, they trusted an aggressive man-to-man press that demanded constant communication and collective coverage. In both phases, the team’s identity depended on players understanding not only their own role, but also how to react when a teammate left his.

The two Champions League finals capture that development clearly. The 2025 final showed PSG at their most dominant, with almost every goal reflecting a different positional relationship. The 2026 final showed that the same freedom could occasionally lose its effectiveness against a compact opponent, but also that PSG were willing to adapt when their first approach failed.

That is what made this PSG side more than a collection of talented individuals. Their greatest strength was the collective understanding that allowed talent to move freely without the team losing its structure. Luis Enrique built the framework first, then gave the players the freedom to transform it.
