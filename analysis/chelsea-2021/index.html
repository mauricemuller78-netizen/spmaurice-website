Chelsea 2020/21: Champions of Europe

Thomas Tuchel's 2020/21 Champions League-winning Chelsea. A tactical analysis of the structures, player roles and decisive ideas behind their European success.

SPMaurice2021 Champions League Winners

In Possession

Under Thomas Tuchel, Chelsea's attacking structure was built around a back five and a highly flexible front three. Depending on the positioning of Mason Mount, the shape could resemble a 5-2-2-1 or a 5-2-1-2, with Mount moving into the central space behind the forwards.

Flexible Front Three and Half-Space Rotations

Chelsea's front three were extremely fluid. The three attacking players could occupy different half-spaces at the same time, but they could also deliberately overload one side by placing two players in the same half-space. That variability made the attack difficult to track and helped Chelsea create local numerical advantages around the opposition's defensive block.

In the build-up, one of the outside centre-backs would often move wide and step outside the penalty area. This gave that defender more time and space on the ball and helped Chelsea bypass the first line of pressure. From there, they could find the wing-back on the same side or play directly over the press with a longer pass towards Kai Havertz.

The box midfield was another key part of Tuchel's structure. By creating four players in central and half-space zones, Chelsea could form multiple passing angles, support combinations around the ball and maintain control while still keeping the wing-backs high and wide.

2021 Champions League Final — Chelsea vs Manchester City

Against Manchester City, Chelsea again used the same broad build-up principles, but Tuchel adapted them specifically to City's aggressive high press.

In Possession — Escaping City's High Press

As in Chelsea's general build-up, one of the outside centre-backs regularly moved wider and outside the penalty area. In the final, César Azpilicueta often took up that position on the right, giving Chelsea an extra outlet away from City's first line of pressure.

Manchester City pressed extremely high. On Chelsea's left, Riyad Mahrez jumped aggressively towards Antonio Rüdiger, which often left Ben Chilwell much freer behind him. Chelsea used that space to create a recurring overload on City's right side, with Chilwell and Mason Mount combining against Kyle Walker. Walker was repeatedly forced to deal with two players and could be overloaded when City's press was broken.

As Chelsea progressed into the final third, Mahrez often dropped deeper into a more defensive position to help Walker deal with that overload. That showed how Chelsea's positioning could force City to alter their own structure and pull an attacking player deeper simply to stabilise the flank.

On the opposite side, Azpilicueta was also important as a distributor. From his wide right-sided position, he frequently looked for Reece James at wing-back, while Chelsea also used more direct passes over City's press towards Kai Havertz or Timo Werner. Those longer balls gave Chelsea a second route out whenever playing short became too risky.

Out of Possession — Defending City's 3-4-3 Diamond

Without the ball, Chelsea had to deal with Manchester City's 3-4-3 diamond. City's structure placed several players between the lines and in the half-spaces, so Chelsea's centre-backs were often asked to step forward aggressively and press the receivers in those areas.

That approach carried an obvious risk: whenever one of Chelsea's centre-backs jumped out of the back line, space opened behind him. City repeatedly tried to attack those gaps with movement from the front line and runs into the channels.

Despite that danger, Chelsea defended the structure extremely well overall. The back line stayed disciplined, the distances between players were usually compact and the team managed to protect the most dangerous central spaces. City could threaten the areas left behind Chelsea's pressing centre-backs, but Tuchel's side remained very secure and stable for most of the match.
